AI Summary
Abstract HexaShell is a coding agent that controls a dual-arm robot via vision and self-written tools, achieving state-of-the-art results on RoboDojo but struggling with contact-heavy tasks. Generated by thinkingmachines/Inkling-Small
▼ Abstract (click to expand)

Generated by thinkingmachines/Inkling-Small Agents built on vision language models (VLMs) now compete with learned policies on robot manipulation leaderboards. We ask how far a coding agent can go when it controls a robot instead of a computer, and where it stops. Our system, HexaShell, keeps the harness thin: it only turns the agent's commands into robot motion. The agent drives a robot with two arms through one command line. It sees three RGB cameras and knows their calibration. After each failed attempt, the agent reads the log, finds the cause, and writes or fixes small programs called tools. In this way it keeps what it learns as code, as a coding agent does when it fixes a failing test. The tools may use only what the robot observes. They use triangulation to find where objects are, relative to each other and to the gripper. If two cameras see the same point, the point lies where the two camera rays meet. If a point lies on a known plane, such as the table or the top of a block, it lies where its ray meets that plane. When the head camera is not enough, the robot moves a wrist camera to get a second view. We build the tools on only ten layouts of one seed per task, then freeze them and test them on ten new layouts of the same seed. We use Codex with GPT-6-Astra at medium reasoning effort. On all 42 RoboDojo tasks, HexaShell reaches a success rate of 39.7% and a progress score of 48.0, averaged in the same way as the leaderboard. This is a new state of the art on RoboDojo. The limit is contact, not reasoning: pushing reaches 92.5% and memory tasks 65.0%, but tight insertion only 8.3%. When the agent fails, it usually sees the failure but cannot make the motion work. We open source the tools evolved so far at https://github.com/HexaFuture/HexaShell.

Generated by thinkingmachines/Inkling-Small GPT-6-Astra demonstrates a level of competence in robotic manipulation and navigation that challenges the conventional paradigm of building specialized models for individual tasks and embodiments. In this report, we ask not only how capable GPT-6-Astra is, but what its capabilities imply for the field. First, we probe the limits of standalone GPT-6-Astra. We conduct a large-scale evaluation across 12 representative benchmarks spanning tabletop manipulation, humanoids, mobile platforms, and dexterous hands. Taken together, our results suggest that GPT-6-Astra has largely crossed the threshold of embodied competence, but not yet that of practical embodied intelligence.

Generated by thinkingmachines/Inkling-Small Decision models answer typed questions about an input with one probability per option, computed in a single forward pass without generating text. Like the fast System 1 of dual-process theory, they answer at once, but their depth is fixed at training time, so they spend the same computation on easy and hard questions. Models that reason in generated text can think longer, but they pay for every extra step in decoded tokens. We present LEV, a decision model that thinks deeper by rerunning its layers in a loop instead of generating tokens. LEV builds on a language model pretrained as a loop, reads the options after every loop step and trains all steps jointly, so one checkpoint can answer at any depth. On three looped backbones, accuracy rises with depth on the same weights, mostly on hard questions, and a 2.6B LEV goes from 63.2% at depth one to 76.2% at depth four on JevBench. On-demand depth, set per question by a small stopper with a threshold fitted on unlabelled requests, saves 15% to 24% of the computation at a cost of under one accuracy point, level with or slightly above a fixed mix of depths. On a locked test set, the 1.4B and 3B models are 5.4 to 8.8 points more accurate than dense (non-looped) models and cascades of the same latency below 50 ms.

Generated by thinkingmachines/Inkling-Small Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards has become a scalable post-training paradigm for large language models. However, training agents over long-horizon interactions presents two challenges: using only terminal rewards makes turn-level credit assignment sparse and high-variance, while sequential mini-batch updates on a fixed rollout batch introduce policy staleness as the current policy drifts from the behavior policy. Existing approaches obtain denser credit via learned critics, process reward models, or on-policy distillation, but they rely on a value network, step-level annotations, or a stronger external teacher that increase training complexity. Meanwhile, importance sampling and clipping mitigate policy drift, but they do not specify a finite target that stops sequential updates from overshooting. In this work, we introduce TreeOPSD, a critic-free, tree-aware posterior self-distillation framework for long-horizon agentic reinforcement learning. Treating each interaction turn as a tree node, TreeOPSD branches alternative continuations from shared prefixes and recursively backs up terminal rewards into implicit turn-level credit. It then maps each credit to a closed-form, bounded teacher distribution parameterized by the likelihood ratio relative to the frozen behavior policy. Minimizing the divergence between this teacher and the student policy recovers the policy-gradient direction as the tangent at the behavior policy while bounding updates toward a finite target across sequential mini-batches. Empirically, TreeOPSD improves agentic capabilities across several competitive benchmarks and model scales, especially for long-horizon tasks, e.g., raising the average accuracy of Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507 by 4.97 points over GRPO on the Deep Research setting.
