HF Page arXiv PDF

Abstract A large-scale corpus of structured proof-supervision records derived from university mathematics textbooks improves language model mathematical reasoning by 16.7 percentage points over a base model when used for fine-tuning. Generated by deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash
▼ Abstract (click to expand)

Generated by deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash Mathematical supervision for language models is typically flattened into isolated problem-solution pairs, discarding the curricular organization and evidential structure of the original sources. We introduce TOME-Math (Textbook-Oriented Mathematical Evidence), a large-scale corpus that converts 2,118 textbooks spanning the core areas of university-level mathematics subjects into approximately 380K structured proof-supervision records while preserving chapter order, proposition-proof links, dependency references, and source provenance. Leveraging these structural signals, we explore curriculum learning across multiple mathematical subjects and characterize scaling patterns with supervision volume and source breadth. At full scale, fine-tuning on TOME-Math raises the unweighted mean score across our held-out evaluations and established public benchmarks by 16.7 percentage points over the base model, while exceeding all three public instruction-data baselines evaluated in aggregate. Our findings highlight structured textbooks as both a scalable source of mathematical supervision and an empirical framework for studying the impact of data organization on mathematical reasoning. We will release the corpus to facilitate further research on structured mathematical supervision.

AI Summary
Abstract Low-bit quantized LLM agents can recover much of their task success through closed-loop guidance from a full-precision teacher, followed by unassisted student training on corrected and reference turns. Generated by deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash
▼ Abstract (click to expand)

Generated by deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash Low-bit quantization reduces the inference cost of LLM agents but can compromise task success. Examining failed rollouts, we find that quantized agents overlook task requirements or environmental conditions, producing environment transitions that diverge from successful full-precision references. Correcting these behavioral divergences remains difficult: offline data may miss the student's failure contexts, while its own rollouts may provide few correct responses in those contexts. We introduce SteerDistill, a quantization-aware distillation method in which a full-precision teacher guides the student to revise failed responses and iteratively refines its guidance using feedback from student execution. This closed-loop teacher steering produces locally verified behavioral corrections. We internalize these corrections by training the student without guidance to match the steered student's predictions at corrected turns and the full-precision teacher's at other turns, enabling unassisted inference. Across five agentic benchmarks and three models, SteerDistill exceeds the strongest quantized baseline by 1.67--2.18 points in average score under W4A16 and stays within one point of BF16. On τ^2-Bench, it outperforms QeRL by 1.84 percentage points in success rate using 67% fewer training GPU hours and produces shorter successful trajectories than the quantized baselines.

Incentivizing Self-Learning in Large Language Models via Reinforcement Learning

Institution: Intern Large Models
Authors: Lingkai Kong, Yuzhe Gu, Haiteng Zhao, Zijian Wu, Youqing Fang
HF Page arXiv PDF
AI Summary
Abstract Large language models (LLMs) draw on parametric knowledge and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to solve knowledge-intensive tasks. Yet problem solving in unfamiliar domains calls for tightly coupled knowledge acquisition and reasoning, beyond factual retrieval and aggregation or learning solely from a preassembled context. We study self-learning -- autonomously retrieving, studying, and applying knowledge from long-form reference materials on the fly. To investigate this capability, we formulate a textbook-based mathematical problem-solving task. LLMs must navigate textbook exposition, learn and apply relevant principles, and construct proofs for exercises, with reference solutions withheld. We introduce a BookNavigator interface to support this human-inspired study process and incentivize self-learning through reinforcement learning (RL) with fine-grained rewards. Experiments show that our method improves performance across in-domain and out-of-domain textbooks and subjects, raising Intern-S2-Preview-35B's overall proof accuracy from 51.5% to 79.5% and outperforming RL-trained textbook-free and fixed-retrieval baselines. These gains extend to complex long-context benchmarks involving different task types, with improvements over the base model of 1.7 and 2.3 percentage points on BrowseComp-Plus and CL-bench, respectively. Together, these findings suggest that RL can foster a domain-agnostic self-learning strategy, highlighting the potential of self-learning from long-form corpora to strengthen broader problem-solving capabilities on complex, long-context tasks.

Institution: Institute of Computing Technology
Authors: Zedong Liu, Jiaan Wu, Xinyang Ma, Le Xu, Kai Wang
HF Page arXiv PDF
AI Summary
Abstract Web-facing AI agents repeatedly access webpages, documents, repositories, and tool responses, yet reading interfaces often expose entire objects when only sparse evidence is needed. This over-reading increases token and latency costs and can dilute relevant evidence. We present SparseRead, a reusable reading control layer that governs content admission without modifying the agent model. Its Read Intent Protocol tracks evolving needs and source coverage; Adaptive Reader Backends select how evidence is located and exposed; and the Budgeted Read Gate bounds cumulative raw exposure per episode with faithful full-view fallback. Ablations validate the three designs, while integration with three agent frameworks demonstrates portability.

Generated by deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash Large language models (LLMs) increasingly support Web-based services that must balance response quality and inference cost across heterogeneous requests. Conventional routing selects one model per prompt, while fixed ensembles cannot adapt their composition and cost to individual requests. We introduce PISA, a prompt-adaptive, cost-aware single-model router, and PISA-E, its extension to ensemble routing. Evolving Infra: Agent-Adaptive LLM Serving at Scale

Institution: MIT HAN Lab
Authors: HaoKang, Hao Kang, Weili Xu, Ziyang Li, Zheng Du
HF Page arXiv PDF
AI Summary
Abstract Agentic workloads are a major source of LLM inference demand. Existing prefill--decode (PD) disaggregated serving systems offer limited flexibility for runtime adaptation of compute allocation and KV cache management, and rely on coarse signals that only partly capture the diverse and dynamic agent workload. These limitations lead to load imbalance between prefill and decode nodes and eviction of KV caches that agents will reuse. We present Evolving Infra, an agent-adaptive serving framework that adapts compute allocation to agent workloads and KV cache management to agent context management at runtime. We decouple compute allocation from key--value (KV) cache management, allowing nodes to switch efficiently between prefill and decode. Building on this design, an online planner matches the PD split to workload composition, while an agent-aware distributed KV manager guides cache eviction and transfer with signals from agent context management. Across coding, math, and computer-use tasks with GLM-5.2 and Kimi K2.6 on up to 256 GB300 GPUs, Evolving Infra reduces mean end-to-end request latency and P95 TTFT by up to 4.1x and 14x over Dynamo, NVIDIA's production framework.

As language models become more capable, their representations are aligning with those of vision models. Authors: armanhatami, Arman Hatami, Romina Aalishah, Ilya E. Monosov

AI Summary
Abstract Subliminal learning can transfer hidden preferences from teacher to student vision-language models through unrelated visual tasks, affecting image choices and explanations. Generated by deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash
▼ Abstract (click to expand)

Generated by deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash Subliminal learning is a phenomenon in large language models (LLMs) where a teacher passes a hidden preference to a student through training data unrelated to that preference. We study whether subliminal learning also occurs in vision-language models (VLMs). We finetune a teacher VLM to prefer a specific animal or facial attribute and use a control teacher from the same base model. Both teachers generate numbers for an unrelated visual task involving geometric shapes. Two students initialized from the same base VLM are finetuned on the same images, using numbers generated by either the biased or control teacher. Across VLMs ranging from 2B to 7B parameters, we find that both animal and facial preferences can transfer through this unrelated task. Students trained on biased-teacher outputs select the target more often than control students, with a difference of up to sim10 percentage points in the animal preference task. Transfer can become stronger with more training data and varies with the target preference, model, and how the numbers are generated. Beyond preferences, we show that the biased student's explanations use more of the same words and phrases as the biased teacher's explanations than those of the control student, even when all three models select the same image. These results matter for training VLMs and show that training on unrelated data may change both a VLM's image preferences and how it explains its choices, and that transfer can become stronger with more training samples.

Generated by thinkingmachines/Inkling-Small Human cognitive tasks let us compare language models with humans under controlled conditions. Yet a model can reproduce the direction of a human effect while making different errors. CogBattery evaluates 54 open-weight language models on text adaptations of 13 cognitive paradigms, comparing each with human data from its source study. Like humans, models respond more to n-back lures than to controls, yet they make far more false alarms on ordinary non-targets. Mixed Signals: Weight-Space Programming for EEG and ECG

Institution: EPFL
Authors: Dara Varam, Taraneh Aminosharieh Najafi, David Atienza
HF Page arXiv PDF
AI Summary
Abstract Weight-space programming allows language models to read a trained neural network's weights and edit them. This opens the door to ``training'' by reasoning rather than through gradient descent. Prior work has demonstrated its functionality and promise for game-playing networks and fixing specific failures in language models, even extending to diffusion models. In this work, we present its applicability to models trained on biosignal data, specifically EEG and ECG data. We show that frontier models are not only able to debug specific faults planted in biosignal-based models without being told what these faults are, but are even capable of writing all the weights of a network from scratch, given only the architecture, the task, and a minimal set of training subjects. We also highlight some of the limitations, which include the inability of language models to improve already well-trained classification models, and their incapacity to reliably recognize, from the weights alone, the task a network was trained on. Overall, our results indicate that weight-space programming can be useful for small networks, such as those deployed on edge devices and wearables, but the concept still requires further investigation before it can be used in practice.

Generated by deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash Different optimizers impose different geometries on parameter updates. Adaptive methods such as Adam and AdamW use coordinate-wise updates, while matrix-aware optimizers such as Muon utilize weight-matrix structure through orthogonalized updates. For Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), however, the effective geometry of its weight matrices is shaped not only by its learned channel-mixing weights, but also by the graph-propagated representations it acts on.
